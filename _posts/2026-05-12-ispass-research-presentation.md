This milestone represents the culmination of my master's thesis research at the Colorado School of Mines under the mentorship of my advisor, Dr. Mehmet Belviranli, in collaboration with professors and student researchers from the University of Rochester and the University of Virginia. ### The Problem: Bridging the Cryogenic Memory-Compute Gap

Superconducting computing using Rapid Single Flux Quantum (RSFQ) logic offers the promise of switching frequencies exceeding tens to hundreds of gigahertz with ultra-low switching energy. However, building practical superconducting computer systems is fundamentally constrained by the **memory-compute gap**:

1. **Cryogenic Thermal Budgets:** RSFQ processors operate at liquid helium temperatures (~4 Kelvin), where cooling power is extremely limited (often mere watts). High-density CMOS memories, on the other hand, generate substantial heat and are typically placed in warmer stages (77 K or room-temperature 300 K).
2. **Physical Interconnects & Signal Conversion:** Transferring data between 4 K RSFQ logic and warmer CMOS memory requires signal level converters (such as Suzuki or SQUID converter stacks) and physical transmission lines routed through the cryostat walls. These interfaces introduce propagation delay, signal conversion rate limits, and thermal parasitic loads that room-temperature simulators do not account for.

Without an end-to-end architectural simulator that models both the compute core and the physical cryostat interconnects, computer architects cannot evaluate how these trade-offs impact realistic program execution.

### What Hibiscus Delivers

To solve this, we built **Hibiscus**, a comprehensive, trace-driven simulation framework that models the complete path from execution core to cryogenic memory:

- **RSFQ Execution Core:** Accurately models the gate-level pipelining behavior of in-order RSFQ processors, executing realistic RV32IMA instruction traces generated by the RISC-V Spike simulator.
- **Hybrid Memory Model:** Integrates CryoMEM- and CACTI-derived access timings for cryogenic CMOS memories placed across 4 K, 77 K, and 300 K temperature zones, alongside support for emerging cryogenic memory technologies.
- **Physical Interconnect Analytics:** Captures temperature-zone hops, converter bandwidth saturation (Suzuki vs. SQUID stacks), cable propagation latencies as a function of physical cryostat dimensions, and channel-count constraints.
- **Configurable Cryogenic Cache Hierarchies:** Evaluates multi-level cache hierarchies, including ultra-small L0 SFQ caches situated directly at 4 K next to the processor core.

### Key Insights Presented at ISPASS

During my presentation at ISPASS, I shared several critical architectural insights surfaced by Hibiscus:

1. **The Power of an Ultra-Small 4 K L0 Cache:** Even a tiny L0 SFQ cache (a few hundred bytes to a few kilobytes) located at 4 K yields substantial speedups—between **1.5x and 4.5x instructions per second (IPS)** depending on workload locality—insulating the fast core from cryostat cable latency.
2. **Interconnect Latency Quickly Dominates:** In systems without a 4 K cache, cable propagation delay across the 4 K-to-77 K interface degrades performance significantly, sometimes cutting overall throughput to half of an idealized zero-delay baseline.
3. **Converter Stack Selection Dictates Read/Write Asymmetry:** SQUID converter stacks provide higher data rates but exhibit pronounced latency asymmetry between memory reads and writes, meaning that workload read/write ratios must guide converter hardware choices.
